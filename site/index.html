Return / $1 is the average value paid back per dollar spent on a ticket bought today; $1.00 or more means the unclaimed prizes are worth more than the unsold tickets. "No jackpot" leaves the top prize out. "vs launch" is the change in return since the game went on sale. "Top prizes" is top prizes left of printed. "Any prize" is the current chance of winning anything. † marks games where the top prize accounts for more than 15¢ of the return. A range under a figure means the state publishes remaining counts for only some prize levels, so the figure is inferred; the range holds the true value with 90% probability. Tap a game for the prize-by-prize breakdown.

Method

Every state lottery publishes, per game, how many prizes at each level were printed and how many are still unclaimed. The overall odds printed on the ticket (for example, 1 in 4.89) give the number of tickets per prize. From those:

  1. Tickets printed = prizes printed × overall odds.
  2. Share unsold = unclaimed ÷ printed, measured on the plentiful low prize levels. Prizes are spread evenly through the print run, so the share of small prizes still unclaimed tracks the share of tickets still unsold.
  3. Return per $1 = value of unclaimed prizes ÷ tickets unsold ÷ ticket price.

Annuity prizes count at their undiscounted total; "for life" prizes use the state's 20-year minimum. Free-ticket prizes count at the ticket price. "Unclaimed" includes sold winners not yet cashed, so new games read slightly high. Tickets stay on sale after the top prizes are gone; the state says so on every game page.

Some states publish remaining counts for only their top prize levels. For those, the unpublished levels are assumed to sell down in step with tickets, which leaves their share of the return exactly where it was at launch; only the published levels move the figure. The share of tickets sold is inferred from the published prizes alone, which are a random sample of the print run, and the range shown is the 90% interval from that sample. A few published prizes give a wide range; thousands give a narrow one. Vermont and Maine publish total unclaimed prize money and percent sold instead of a prize table, and their return is computed from those two figures directly.

None of this is advice. On average every game here pays back less than it costs.
